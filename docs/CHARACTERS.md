# Characters

The core cast of the story-based learning layer. They appear only where the moment is big enough to deserve them: key ideas, turning points, things that changed the world. Most stories belong to someone else (Maya the baker, Silas and his lily) or to no one at all (a duck, a boat, a clock tower). A reader should never feel introduced to a crowd.

## The clock

Characters age faster than the learner, so a student who starts in pre-K meets them as small children and, by high school, as adults with families. Each band is a chapter:

## The people

- **Mike**, the skeptic. A street-hardened but warm man with a rough-edged childhood, not the worst, not the best. Physical work taught him to build, fix, and keep going, and, the hard way, to reframe failure, handle big feelings, and stop keeping up with anyone. Thinks like a fallibilist: hold ideas loosely, test them. Calisthenics, gymnastics, cycling, music. Bald with a close dark beard as an adult. Frederick taught him the scientific method; they become friends. When science fails him or life gets hard, he goes to Georgette.
- **Chloe**, the artist. Bright, patterned, layered clothes. Every kind of art. Wants to see the world and have new experiences, and does. The one who notices what things look like.
- **Frederick**, the scientist. His dad's digital microscope started it at seven. Cellular and molecular biology, then a professorship. By his late thirties, a professor, his short hair and large handlebar mustache have gone gray early (Mikey's answer to Q34, pass MI: the age his Wonder portrait shows, beside Mike and Chloe in their thirties); as an old man the same mustache, and always a white lab coat. Introduces experiment and method to Mike.
- **Georgette**, the grandparent of faith. Not related by blood: the godmother of Mike's uncle, and so a fixture of Mike's childhood. Short auburn hair, a red coat, a book-filled study with a round window and a teacup. Well read, cultured, wise. Faith is part of who she is and never a sermon; she gives advice, comfort, and, when asked, prayer. Her working life (Mikey, pass MF, Q15: "a history teacher only"; pass MG: "Georgette works up to history teacher through her fascination with history"): history fascinated her from girlhood, first in her grandmother's stories and then in the piece of the town history she wrote as a young woman (reading-12), and it carried her the whole way: she studied for a degree at a university (reading-college) and worked her way up from teacher's aide, earning her teaching license along the way, to a history classroom of her own, where she taught for forty years, most of them to sixth-graders. In retirement she volunteers, runs a library club and serves as a museum docent. She was never a nurse. A story that shows her teaching shows the teacher she worked to become, and philosophy-3 and reading-12 say how she got there.
- **Savanah**, Mike's wife. Started with nothing in a small farm town where she learned the hard skills of life. Wanted more, went to the city, and became powerful and influential there. Copper-red hair. Her clothes follow her path: a work shirt on the farm, a navy polo with a plain badge in customer service at the medical equipment company, a cardigan at the clinic's front desk, and a smart coat from her late twenties, once she studies engineering. Their two children come along in the adult chapter. Her work (Mikey, pass MF, Q13: "No, she is a CTO for a medical equipment company."; pass MG: "Savanah isn't always CTO.  She gets there through hard work and starts in customer service."): she earns every step. She works two summers as a junior camp counselor, at seventeen and eighteen (health-6, philosophy-6), and leaves the farm for the city with a print shop job and her egg money and pays for a first year of community college from the jar marked college (econ-6). Her first job at a company that builds medical equipment, ultrasound machines among them, is in customer service, answering calls from clinics and going along with the company's trainer to learn the machines from the people who use them (sexual-health-6). She manages a clinic's front office before she ever studies engineering (psychology-9), starts an engineering program at a community college at twenty-seven (psychology-college), transfers to a university to finish her engineering degree, and comes back to the company as an engineer, working her way up until, in the adult chapter, she is its chief technology officer. Along the way she runs a market stall and leads a city transit project. No story makes her the chief technology officer before the adult chapter (the stories test holds it), and she was never a sonographer.
- **Jaxon**, six, Mike and Savanah's son. Sensitive; cares for everyone in need. Sandy light-brown hair, a green checkered shirt.
- **Harlow**, three, their daughter. Mischievous, adorable, very sharp. Blonde curls in pigtails with sage-green bows.

## The Wonder voices are the cast grown up

The four Wonder voices are Frederick, Chloe, Georgette and Mike speaking as grown people, each from the way of seeing they live by (pass LY), while the stories meet Mike, Chloe and Frederick as small children first. Mikey (pass LZ): "I'm okay with the grown voices for wonder questions. It's like a sophisticated story that has flashbacks to their childhood to show how they developed the mindset they have today. All of the little experiences they had throughout life led them to either become a scientist, artist, grandparent of faith or skeptic. It's one of the beautifully unique aspects of this app."

What this means for the writing (Claude's reading in pass LZ, confirmed by Mikey in pass MG, Q3: "yes, it's good and your someth more idea is did good too"): a Wonder voice speaks from its person's grown mindset, and a core-cast story, at any age, can show one of the small experiences that built it, such as Frederick's first look through his dad's microscope at seven, Chloe noticing what things look like, or three-year-old Mike working out where his ball rolled (The Ball That Rolled Away). And the something more he approved: a later story can show a grown core character, Savanah too, remembering a moment an earlier story shows, one line at the right turn, never a retelling. The first two (the story check, pass MG): in speech-6 (grade 6) grown Georgette's fast-read lemonade steps fail, and she remembers learning how instructions go wrong as a girl, when her grandmother followed her letter on folding a paper boat and made a paper hat (writing-3, grade 3); in psychology-college Savanah expects a semester of reading about other people's experiments, having once tested two signs in a clinic's waiting room (psychology-9). A memory comes after the story that shows its moment, in grade order, and agrees with that story and with the clock. Backstory is something else: philosophy-3 (grade 3) opens on Georgette's, the history she has loved since her grandmother's stories and her climb from teacher's aide, and reading-12 (grade 12) shows the town history that caught her for good.

## Rules

1. Savanah, Jaxon and Harlow appear only in wholesome scenes: no conflict aimed at them, no scenes that invite criticism of how they live, nothing a parent would wince at. Mike, Chloe, Frederick and Georgette can carry harder stories.
2. Georgette is not Mike's grandmother. She is the godmother of his uncle.
3. The core cast gets the most significant modules: the ideas that changed the world. Everyone else gets everyone else.
4. Who meets whom, and when (Mikey's answer to Q41, pass MK: "we can remove the restriction and you can choose what makes sense as you write considering the general chronological order.  This gives you more freedom and flexibility with the stories."): a story brings in as many of the core cast as it needs, chosen as it is written, in keeping with the general chronological order of the clock above, so who knows whom, and at what age, stays true across the grades. Until pass MK this rule read "Introduce at most one new core character per story, and never all of them at once. Across a band a student should meet the cast the way you meet neighbors, one at a time."; pre-K 3's four long stories, where Chloe, Mike and Frederick picnic with their teacher and Mike goes to the park with Georgette, were its question.
5. Faith stays respectful and personal to Georgette; no story argues for or against religion.
6. Every scene with a core character names its character sheet (C serial) in docs/ART-REQUESTS.md so they look the same everywhere.
7. Ages and relatives in the story text (Mikey, pass MB): "I think the ages and relatives are okay to mention in stories but keep the age part at a minimum.  It's actually good to state the age periodically just not consistently.  Relatives, no restriction anymore." A story may name a core character's relatives, and may state an age now and then, never as a habit (the stories test holds the counts). A stated age fits the clock above or is plainly a flashback to a younger age, and agrees with every other story about that person; docs/CAST-AGES.md lists every age the stories state, beside the clock.
